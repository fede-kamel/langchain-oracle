## **9. ACUTE TREATMENT**

The acute treatment of a multiple sclerosis relapse is aimed at reducing the inflammation in the central nervous system, speeding up the recovery from the relapse, and improving the patient's symptoms. The standard of care for the treatment of an acute MS relapse is a short course of high-dose corticosteroids. In cases of severe, steroid-refractory relapses, plasma exchange (PLEX) may be considered.

**High-Dose Corticosteroids**

High-dose corticosteroids are the mainstay of treatment for acute MS relapses. They are thought to work by reducing inflammation, decreasing the permeability of the blood-brain barrier, and suppressing the immune response. The most commonly used corticosteroid for the treatment of MS relapses is intravenous methylprednisolone (IVMP).

*   **Dosage and Administration:** The standard dose of IVMP is 1000 mg administered intravenously once a day for three to five days. The infusion is typically given over a period of one to two hours. Some neurologists may follow the IVMP with a tapering course of oral prednisone, but the benefit of this practice is not well-established.
*   **Efficacy:** Clinical trials have shown that high-dose corticosteroids can shorten the duration and severity of an MS relapse. However, there is no convincing evidence that they have a long-term beneficial effect on the course of the disease or the prevention of disability.
*   **Side Effects:** High-dose corticosteroids can be associated with a number of side effects, including:
    *   **Short-term side effects:** Mood changes (euphoria, depression, psychosis), insomnia, metallic taste, stomach upset, and an increased risk of infection.
    *   **Long-term side effects (with repeated use):** Osteoporosis, avascular necrosis of the hip, cataracts, and an increased risk of cardiovascular disease.

Given the severity of this patient's relapse, with significant motor, sensory, and cerebellar deficits, a course of high-dose IVMP is clearly indicated. A typical regimen would be 1000 mg of IVMP per day for five days. The patient should be monitored for potential side effects, and she should be counseled on the importance of reporting any new or worsening symptoms.

**Plasma Exchange (PLEX)**

Plasma exchange, or plasmapheresis, is a procedure in which the patient's blood is removed, the plasma is separated from the blood cells, and the blood cells are returned to the patient with a replacement fluid (such as albumin or fresh frozen plasma). The rationale for using PLEX in MS is that it removes circulating antibodies, complement proteins, and other inflammatory mediators that may be contributing to the relapse.

*   **Indications:** PLEX is typically reserved for patients with severe, disabling relapses that have not responded to a course of high-dose corticosteroids. It is most effective for relapses that are characterized by prominent motor weakness.
*   **Dosage and Administration:** A typical course of PLEX consists of five to seven exchanges performed over a period of 10 to 14 days.
*   **Efficacy:** Several small, randomized controlled trials have shown that PLEX can be effective in improving the outcome of severe, steroid-refractory MS relapses.
*   **Side Effects:** PLEX is an invasive procedure that requires a large-bore intravenous catheter. Potential side effects include hypotension, citrate toxicity (from the anticoagulant used in the procedure), and an increased risk of infection.

In this patient, PLEX would be a consideration if she fails to show a significant improvement after a course of high-dose IVMP. Given the severity of her gait and cerebellar ataxia, she would be a good candidate for PLEX if her relapse proves to be refractory to steroids.

**Other Acute Treatments**

*   **ACTH (Adrenocorticotropic Hormone):** ACTH is a hormone that stimulates the adrenal glands to produce cortisol. It has been shown to be effective in the treatment of MS relapses, but it is more expensive and has a less favorable side effect profile than high-dose corticosteroids. It is rarely used as a first-line treatment.
*   **Rehabilitation:** Physical and occupational therapy are an essential part of the management of an acute MS relapse. Rehabilitation can help to improve strength, mobility, and function, and it can help the patient to regain as much independence as possible.

## **10. DISEASE-MODIFYING THERAPY**

The initiation of a disease-modifying therapy (DMT) is a critical step in the long-term management of a patient with relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis. The goal of DMT is to reduce the frequency and severity of relapses, delay the progression of disability, and reduce the accumulation of new lesions on MRI. There are now more than 20 DMTs approved for the treatment of MS, with a wide range of mechanisms of action, efficacy, and side effect profiles. The choice of a DMT should be individualized based on the patient's disease activity, comorbidities, and preferences.

Given the high level of disease activity in this patient (frequent relapses, enhancing lesions on MRI), a high-efficacy DMT would be the most appropriate choice. The DMTs can be broadly categorized into three groups based on their efficacy:

**1. Injectable Therapies (Lower Efficacy)**

These were the first DMTs to be approved for the treatment of MS. They are generally considered to be less effective than the newer oral and infusion therapies, but they have a long track record of safety.

*   **Interferon-beta (Avonex, Betaseron, Plegridy, Rebif):** Interferons are naturally occurring proteins that have immunomodulatory effects. They are thought to work by reducing inflammation and inhibiting the migration of immune cells into the CNS. They are administered by subcutaneous or intramuscular injection.
*   **Glatiramer Acetate (Copaxone, Glatopa):** Glatiramer acetate is a synthetic polypeptide that is thought to work by mimicking myelin basic protein and inducing the production of anti-inflammatory immune cells. It is administered by subcutaneous injection.

**2. Oral Therapies (Moderate to High Efficacy)**

The oral DMTs offer the convenience of a pill, but they are associated with a greater risk of side effects than the injectable therapies.

*   **Fingolimod (Gilenya):** Fingolimod is a sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) receptor modulator that works by trapping lymphocytes in the lymph nodes, preventing them from entering the CNS. It is a once-daily pill.
*   **Siponimod (Mayzent):** Siponimod is another S1P receptor modulator that is approved for the treatment of both relapsing and secondary progressive MS.
*   **Ozanimod (Zeposia):** Ozanimod is a newer S1P receptor modulator with a more selective mechanism of action, which may lead to a lower risk of side effects.
*   **Teriflunomide (Aubagio):** Teriflunomide is a pyrimidine synthesis inhibitor that works by inhibiting the proliferation of activated lymphocytes. It is a once-daily pill.
*   **Dimethyl Fumarate (Tecfidera):** Dimethyl fumarate is an immunomodulatory agent that is thought to work by activating the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway. It is a twice-daily pill.
*   **Diroximel Fumarate (Vumerity):** Diroximel fumarate is a newer formulation of a fumarate that is designed to have a lower risk of gastrointestinal side effects than dimethyl fumarate.
*   **Cladribine (Mavenclad):** Cladribine is a purine nucleoside analog that works by depleting lymphocytes. It is administered in two short courses of oral treatment over two years.

**3. Infusion Therapies (High Efficacy)**

The infusion DMTs are generally considered to be the most effective therapies for MS, but they are also associated with the most significant risks.

*   **Natalizumab (Tysabri):** Natalizumab is a monoclonal antibody that works by blocking the migration of lymphocytes across the blood-brain barrier. It is highly effective, but it is associated with a risk of progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML), a rare but serious brain infection.
*   **Ocrelizumab (Ocrevus):** Ocrelizumab is a monoclonal antibody that works by depleting B cells. It is administered by intravenous infusion every six months. It is highly effective and is approved for the treatment of both relapsing and primary progressive MS.
*   **Ofatumumab (Kesimpta):** Ofatumumab is another B-cell depleting monoclonal antibody that is administered by subcutaneous injection once a month.
*   **Alemtuzumab (Lemtrada):** Alemtuzumab is a monoclonal antibody that works by depleting both B cells and T cells. It is administered in two courses of intravenous infusion over two years. It is highly effective, but it is associated with a risk of serious autoimmune side effects.
